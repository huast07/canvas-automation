Photon flux and detector geometry

PHY293 · Waves and Modern Physics · Light and atoms

Why this matters

Convert optical power to a photon rate before applying an area fraction.

Scope: confirmed

From first principles

Level 1 · Power is energy per second. Divide by the energy of one photon to find the number of photons per second.

Assumptions

Assume nearly monochromatic radiation, isotropic emission, a small detector normal to the ray direction, and no losses unless specified. Use only the power in the stated spectral band.

Reasoning / derivation

Level 2 · N˙=P/Eγ=Pλ/(hc),N˙det=N˙A4πr2. If each collected photon produces one electron, the ideal photocurrent is I=eN˙det; actual efficiency may reduce it.

Example

Level 3 · PS6 Q5 uses 3 W of visible power, not the bulb’s 60 W electrical rating. At 550 nm, N˙≃8.3×1018s−1. A 1 mm diameter pupil at 3 m intercepts roughly 5.8×1010s−1.
Common trap: A quoted diameter must be halved before computing area.

PROBLEM TYPE

1. Photon counts and thermal radiation

Level 1 · Power is energy per second. Divide by the energy of one photon to find the number of photons per second.

Reasoning / derivation

Level 2 · N˙=P/Eγ=Pλ/(hc),N˙det=N˙A4πr2. If each collected photon produces one electron, the ideal photocurrent is I=eN˙det; actual efficiency may reduce it.

Worked example

Author-created example: a source emits 1.00 mW at 620 nm isotropically. How many photons per second cross a 1.00 cm² detector at 1.00 m?

  1. Each photon has E=1240/620=2.00eV=3.204×10−19J.
  2. The source rate is 10−3/(3.204×10−19)=3.12×1015s−1.
  3. The detector fraction is 10−4/(4π)=7.96×10−6. The detected rate is about 2.48×1010s−1, before efficiency losses.
