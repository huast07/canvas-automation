START HERE · QUIZ 2

One framework for most problems

Sketch the physical system → identify what is fixed → write an energy balance → build the thermal network → solve → check.

Begin with problem types 1: physical reasoning and 2: walls and surface temperatures. Then move to 4: pipes, 5: spheres and phase change, and 6: radiation. Use the map and individual concept pages when you need a foundation explained. Use the worked examples first and follow concept links when you need more detail.

The key distinction: at fixed hot and cold temperatures, more thermal resistance means less heat flow. At fixed electrical power, steady heat output stays equal to that power, and more resistance means a hotter device.

Outside the quiz: thermodynamics, fins, thermal diffusivity and later transient-conduction methods. They remain in the full course map for context. Basic energy-storage balances from Week 7 are still included.

QUESTION CATEGORY 01

1 · Predict changes before calculating

Identify what the problem holds fixed, then predict the response. Evidence: 2024 MC1–3; 2025 MC1–3.

Steady energy balance

Steady state means no energy accumulation with time. It does not require uniform temperature or zero heat flow.

Reasoning / derivation. For a stationary solid, Q˙in+Pelec−Q˙out=dUdt. At steady state the right side is zero. For an unheated layer with no storage, Q˙in=Q˙out. With thermal generation in a solid, use q˙genV instead of double-counting its energy source.

Thermal conductivity and material trends

Thermal conductivity k, in W/(m·K), measures how readily a material carries heat for a specified temperature gradient. It is a material property; convection coefficient h describes a surface–flow situation.

Series resistances

In series, the same heat rate passes through successive layers and boundaries. Temperature drops add.

Reasoning / derivation. Each drop is ΔTi=Q˙Ri. Summing gives Thot−Tcold=Q˙∑iRi,Rtotal=∑iRi. When endpoints are air temperatures, include convection boundaries. When endpoints are specified surface temperatures, do not add imaginary convection resistances beyond them.

QUESTION CATEGORY 04

4 · Insulated cylindrical pipe

Use radius-dependent area and logarithmic shell resistance. Evidence: Lecture 25 pp.8–12; Week 8 Q3; 2024 Problem 1.

Cylindrical conduction

Radial heat crosses cylindrical surfaces of area A(r)=2πrL. Area increases outward, so the plane-wall formula with a single arbitrary area is not exact.

Reasoning / derivation. Fourier law gives Q˙=−k(2πrL)dT/dr. Integrating from inner to outer radius gives Rcyl=ln(ro/ri)2πkL,Q˙=Ti−ToRcyl. Convection areas are 2πriL and 2πroL.

Radial temperature profiles and flux

Steady radial conduction conserves total heat rate through an unheated shell, while flux falls outward as the available area grows.

Reasoning / derivation. For a cylinder, T(r)=Ti+(To−Ti)ln(r/ri)ln(ro/ri). For a sphere replace the logarithmic ratio by (1/ri−1/r)/(1/ri−1/ro). Thus the profiles are logarithmic and inverse-radius, not straight lines. Cylinder flux varies as 1/r; sphere flux as 1/r2.

Radiation, emissivity and absorption

A blackbody emits σT4 per unit area. A real surface emits εσT4, where emissivity 0≤ε≤1. Absorptivity describes the fraction of incident radiation absorbed.

Reasoning / derivation. For the enclosure model, subtract absorbed surroundings radiation from emission: Q˙rad=εσA(Ts4−Tsurr4). Visible white does not imply low infrared emissivity; Lecture 20 lists white paint near 0.90.

Lecture 19

Heat, heat rate and heat flux

Heat Q is energy transferred because of a temperature difference. Heat rate Q˙ measures joules transferred each second; flux q′′ measures rate per unit area.
Assumptions: Use the area actually crossed by heat. A uniform flux is needed to replace an area integral by a product.

Reasoning / derivation. Q˙=dQdt,Q˙=∫Aq′′dA. For uniform flux, q′′=Q˙/A. For constant heat rate, Q=Q˙t. Units are J, W=J/s, and W/m².

Example. Original example: 100 W crosses 2 m² for 60 s. Flux is 50 W/m2, while transferred energy is 6000 J.

Thermal conductivity and material trends

Thermal conductivity k, in W/(m·K), measures how readily a material carries heat for a specified temperature gradient. It is a material property; convection coefficient h describes a surface–flow situation.
Assumptions: Use properties at a representative temperature when the constant-property approximation is reasonable. Trends are not universal rankings of all solids, liquids and gases.

Reasoning / derivation. From q′′=−kdT/dx, fixed flux implies |dT/dx|=|q′′|/k. Metals generally conduct much better than gases and insulation; alloys often conduct less well than pure constituents. Temperature dependence differs among materials. Interpolation: k(T)=ka+(kb−ka)(T−Ta)/(Tb−Ta).

Example. Original example: at q′′=1000 W/m2, materials with k=400 and k=0.04 have gradient magnitudes 2.5 and 25,000 K/m. Large conductivity means a small gradient at the same flux.

Fourier law and its sign

Fourier law relates heat flowing in a chosen direction to temperature change along that direction: Q˙x=−kAdT/dx.
Assumptions: Local isotropic conduction; the 1D expression assumes temperature varies principally along one coordinate.

Reasoning / derivation. If temperature falls as x increases, dT/dx<0. The minus sign then makes Q˙x>0: heat travels toward increasing x, from hot to cold. For steady conduction through a slab with constant k and A and no generation, integrate: Q˙L=−kA(T2−T1).

Example. Original example: k=0.5, A=2 m2, L=0.1 m, surfaces 40°C and 20°C. Heat rate from the 40°C face to the 20°C face is 200 W.

Lectures 19–21

Choose the heat-transfer modes

Conduction transfers energy through microscopic interactions; convection exchanges heat between a surface and moving fluid; radiation transfers electromagnetic energy and can cross a vacuum.
Assumptions: Choose modes for each region and boundary. An opaque solid interior is normally modelled by conduction. A gas gap may have conduction, circulation and radiation.

Reasoning / derivation. Trace the physical path. Through a wall: conduction. At a wall exposed to air: convection, often radiation as a second path. Across an evacuated gap: radiation remains. A convection coefficient already includes the near-wall conductive transport in its fluid model; do not automatically add a second gas-conduction path for the same process.

Example. Original example: a hot solid plate in a vacuum still loses energy by radiation. Removing air removes convection, not radiation.

Lecture 21

Steady energy balance

Steady state means no energy accumulation with time. It does not require uniform temperature or zero heat flow.
Assumptions: Define the system boundary and account for all relevant heat, work and mass-energy transfers. For a solid with electrical input, count electrical power once.

Reasoning / derivation. For a stationary solid, Q˙in+Pelec−Q˙out=dUdt. At steady state the right side is zero. For an unheated layer with no storage, Q˙in=Q˙out. With thermal generation in a solid, use q˙genV instead of double-counting its energy source.

Example. Week 7 Q2: imposed flux is 20 W/m², but convection removes 20(50−30)=400 W/m2. Net storage is −380 W/m2: the wall cools. It is not steady.

Plane-wall temperature profiles

Temperature profiles show where the temperature changes most strongly and which material offers more resistance.
Assumptions: A straight line within a plane layer requires steady 1D conduction, constant area and conductivity, and no internal heat generation. Different layers may have different slopes.

Combined convection and radiation

A surface can exchange heat with air and surrounding walls simultaneously. Their heat rates add because they are separate paths from the surface.
Assumptions: Only combine coefficients into one temperature difference when bulk-fluid and radiative-surroundings temperatures are equal. Do not add radiation again if a provided h is already combined.

Reasoning / derivation. Factor Ts4−Tsurr4=(Ts−Tsurr)(Ts+Tsurr)(Ts2+Tsurr2). Thus hrad=εσ(Ts+Tsurr)(Ts2+Tsurr2). If T∞=Tsurr, Q˙=(h+hrad)A(Ts−T∞). This factorization is exact for the model, but h_rad depends on unknown T_s; solve or iterate consistently.

Lecture 20

Convection and the coefficient h

Convection uses the solid surface temperature Ts and bulk fluid temperature T∞: Q˙=hAs(Ts−T∞). The coefficient h combines flow, geometry and fluid-property effects.
Assumptions: Use the exposed surface area. Forced convection uses externally driven flow; natural convection uses buoyancy. A fan usually changes h; adding area does not by itself specify a new h.

Reasoning / derivation. Define h=Q˙/[As(Ts−T∞)]. Its units are W/(m²·K). Rearrangement gives Ts=T∞+Q˙/(hAs), which makes surface cooling at fixed power explicit.

Example. Original example: 60 W leaves a 0.2 m² surface into 20°C air with h=10. Then Ts=20+60/(10×0.2)=50 ∘C.

Radiation, emissivity and absorption

A blackbody emits σT4 per unit area. A real surface emits εσT4, where emissivity 0≤ε≤1. Absorptivity describes the fraction of incident radiation absorbed.
Assumptions: Temperatures in fourth powers must be kelvin. The simple net-exchange formula applies to a small surface in a large isothermal enclosure; parallel black plates also use the blackbody difference. General two-body exchange requires more geometry and surface information.

Reasoning / derivation. For the enclosure model, subtract absorbed surroundings radiation from emission: Q˙rad=εσA(Ts4−Tsurr4). Lectures 22–23

Thermal resistance and its units

Thermal resistance measures temperature difference needed per watt of heat transfer: R=ΔT/Q˙, with units K/W.
Assumptions: Use a law valid for the chosen geometry and conditions. A resistance based on radiation can depend on temperature. Area-normalized resistance has different units.

Reasoning / derivation. Rearrange each law into Q˙=ΔT/R: Rwall=LkA,Rconv=1hA,Rrad=1hradA. The heat-flux version uses R′′ in m²·K/W and q′′=ΔT/R′′. For a uniform plane area, R′′=RA.

Example. Original example: a wall with L=0.1, k=0.5, A=2 has R=0.1 K/W. A 20 K surface difference drives 200 W.

Series resistances

In series, the same heat rate passes through successive layers and boundaries. Temperature drops add.
Assumptions: Steady state, no internal source or side loss along the series path. Every resistance must use its own correct area.

Reasoning / derivation. Each drop is ΔTi=Q˙Ri. Summing gives Thot−Tcold=Q˙∑iRi,Rtotal=∑iRi. When endpoints are air temperatures, include convection boundaries. When endpoints are specified surface temperatures, do not add imaginary convection resistances beyond them.

Surface temperatures and dominant resistance

After finding heat rate, recover each surface or interface temperature by subtracting the temperature drop across each resistance along the direction of heat flow.
Assumptions: Use the local branch heat rate. Temperatures lie between hot and cold reservoirs in a passive network with no source; this check can fail if a source is present.

Contact resistance and conductance

Real contacting solids touch at microscopic spots, leaving gaps. A finite interface temperature jump is therefore possible even when the interface is treated as geometrically thin.

Cylindrical conduction

Radial heat crosses cylindrical surfaces of area A(r)=2πrL. Area increases outward, so the plane-wall formula with a single arbitrary area is not exact.

Radial temperature profiles and flux

Steady radial conduction conserves total heat rate through an unheated shell, while flux falls outward as the available area grows.
Assumptions: Constant k, steady radial flow, no shell generation. The formula does not describe temperature inside a heat-generating solid core.

Reasoning / derivation. For a cylinder, T(r)=Ti+(To−Ti)ln(r/ri)ln(ro/ri). For a sphere replace the logarithmic ratio by (1/ri−1/r)/(1/ri−1/ro). Thus the profiles are logarithmic and inverse-radius, not straight lines. Cylinder flux varies as 1/r; sphere flux as 1/r2.

Example. Original example: cylinder surfaces at 100°C and 0°C, with radii 1 and 4 cm. At 2 cm, the logarithmic fraction is ln2/ln4=1/2, giving 50°C. Flux at 4 cm is one quarter of flux at 1 cm.

Sensible heating and phase change

Sensible heating changes temperature; latent heating changes phase. Heat transfer determines the energy-delivery rate, which can then be linked to temperature change, evaporation or melting time.

Temperature change from energy imbalance

An energy imbalance changes stored thermal energy. A nearly uniform-temperature body can have its instantaneous heating or cooling rate related directly to that imbalance.
