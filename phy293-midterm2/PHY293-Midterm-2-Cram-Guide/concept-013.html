Transitions, spectral lines and ionization

PHY293 · Waves and Modern Physics · Light and atoms

Why this matters

Convert energy differences between allowed levels into photons.

Scope: confirmed

From first principles

Level 1 · An emission moves the atom from a higher energy to a lower energy. The photon takes the positive difference. Ionization lifts the bound particle to the zero-energy continuum.

Assumptions

For an electron in a hydrogen-like ion, ground state is n=1, first excited state n=2, and fifth excited state n=6. Use the correct particle mass and nuclear charge.

Reasoning / derivation

Level 2 · Eγ=Ei−Ef=13.6Z2(1nf2−1ni2)eV(ni>nf). Then λ=hc/Eγ. Ionization from n costs 13.6Z2/n2eV. Absorption requires a matching allowed energy difference.

Example

Level 3 · He+ falling from n=2 to n=1 emits 40.8 eV, with wavelength about 30.4 nm. If it hits a metal with work function 6.8 eV, the maximum electron kinetic energy is 34.0 eV. This chain appears in 2025 Question I.
Common trap: The negative sign of a bound energy does not make emitted photon energy negative.

PROBLEM TYPE

2. Atomic transition → photoelectric emission

Level 1 · An emission moves the atom from a higher energy to a lower energy. The photon takes the positive difference. Ionization lifts the bound particle to the zero-energy continuum.

Reasoning / derivation

Level 2 · Eγ=Ei−Ef=13.6Z2(1nf2−1ni2)eV(ni>nf). Then λ=hc/Eγ. Ionization from n costs 13.6Z2/n2eV. Absorption requires a matching allowed energy difference.

Worked example

2025 Midterm I(a–b): He+ emits in the n=2→1 transition; the metal stopping potential is 34.0 V. Find wavelength and work function.

  1. Eγ=13.6(2)2(1−1/4)=40.8eV.
  2. λ=1240/40.8≃30.4nm.
  3. Kmax=eVs=34.0eV, so ϕ=40.8−34.0=6.8eV. The stopping potential describes the electron, not the photon.
